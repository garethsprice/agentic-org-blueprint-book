= What Is Shape, Steer, Do?

[.lead]
_Three kinds of work, and which ones to hand to an agent_

[.in-short]
*In short:* Shape, Steer, Do is a model of the three kinds of work in any organisation: shaping what it becomes, steering how it runs so results are predictable, and doing the work itself. In Gareth Price's _The Blueprint_ it guides delegation: leaders keep Shape, set Steer's standards, and hand the Do to the role that owns it.

== Three Kinds of Work, Done by the Same People and Agents

[cols="1,2,2,2",options="header"]
|===
| |Shape |Steer |Do

|The question it answers
|What should this become, and why?
|How do we make the results predictable?
|What needs doing now?

|What it produces
|The aim, the destination, the roles, the bets
|Standards, plans, checks, reviews, follow-up
|The reply, the report, the board, the code

|What a leader should do with it
|Keep it; use agents to research and draft options
|Set the standards; delegate the running of them
|Delegate nearly all of it, to the role that owns it
|===

The model adapts Michael Gerber's entrepreneur, manager and technician. It describes kinds of work rather than kinds of people, because a single person, or a single agent, does all three at different moments.

Agents can contribute to all three. A project-management agent told to treat "WILL SLIP" as "an INTERVENTION trigger, not a status note" is doing Steer work well. But Shape is the work only an organisation's leaders can own, and setting standards is close behind. Everything else can be delegated. The question is to whom: not "my agent", but the role that owns the work.

== Why It Matters: Agents Pull Leaders Back Into Do

Until recently a leader who wanted to do an individual contributor's job had to find the time to do it badly, so leaders delegated Do. Agents have made doing almost free. A chief executive can now rebuild a dashboard or draft a working code change in an evening, without asking anyone.

That creates a trap. A leader's output is the output of the organisation they run. An agent that produces a hundred useful-looking drafts which three engineers then spend a week checking has subtracted from that output, while making the leader feel more productive than ever. Worse, when the Do already belongs to someone else, the leader's agent becomes a way around the org chart.

Individual contributors face the mirror image. When agents take on their Do, their job moves towards Steer: writing the spec, briefing the agents and owning the verification bar. Their expertise moves into the checks.

== Example: A Customer Success Function

In one customer success function, the three layers look like this. Shape is deciding what the function is for and which roles it needs. Steer is a written bar such as a seven-day shadow period before a customer success agent may send anything on its own. Do is the agent drafting a reply to a customer's question.

The fictional company in xref:appendix-c-worked-example.adoc[Appendix C] puts the model on its scorecard. Its Head of Customer Success spent about 10% of her week on Shape work. The target, once agents took on more of the Do, is half her week on onboarding, playbooks and product feedback.

== How to Start This Week

. Scroll back through your last ten requests to Claude, ChatGPT or Grok and paste them into a new conversation.
. Ask it to label each one Shape, Steer or Do, say whether you described the outcome or dictated the method, and flag any that would normally belong to someone else.
. Take the request that most looks like someone else's Do. Rewrite it as a short brief to the role that owns it: what you want, why it matters, and how you will know it's done.
. Send that brief in a shared channel.
. Block an hour this week for Shape work only, with an agent researching options for one decision only you can make.

== How It Differs From Doer and Manager

The familiar split is between doers and managers: two kinds of people, two job titles. Shape, Steer, Do splits the work, not the people. It adds Shape, which the doer-and-manager split leaves out, and it applies to agents as well as people. With agents, the useful question is not whether someone is a doer or a manager, but which layer of work they are delegating, and to whom.

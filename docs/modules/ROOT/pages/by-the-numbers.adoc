:page-faq-1-q: Do multi-agent systems make more mistakes than a single agent?
:page-faq-1-a: They can, depending on how they are organised. A Google Research study of 180 agent configurations found that agents working in parallel without coordination amplified errors 17.2 times, against 4.4 times when an orchestrator checked their work. Structure also depends on the task: coordination improved work that splits into parallel parts by 80.9%, while every multi-agent variant degraded strictly sequential work by 39 to 70%. Chapter 6 of The Blueprint covers the study and what it means for reporting lines.
:page-faq-2-q: How quickly do AI agents multiply in a small company?
:page-faq-2-a: Faster than anyone tracks them. At CorralData, about ten team bots appeared in Slack within two weeks of adopting Grok Bot, most not built by engineers, and by the end of the first day two QA bots built by different people were reviewing the same work. The author's own workstation ran fourteen agent profiles, one of them an empty shell nobody had removed. These figures come from one company, described in the Introduction and Chapters 10 and 17, and are a case, not a survey.
:page-faq-3-q: Is it worth using several AI agents instead of one?
:page-faq-3-a: Sometimes, at a price. Anthropic reported that its multi-agent research system outperformed a single agent by 90.2% on an internal research test, while using about fifteen times more tokens than chats. A 2026 study by Xiaoxiao Li found that a single agent choosing from a library of skills stayed competitive with multi-agent systems while using 53.7% fewer tokens and finishing 49.5% faster, though its accuracy at choosing the right skill degraded once the library grew past about thirty skills. Chapter 5 of The Blueprint weighs the two.
:page-faq-4-q: Do people actually check what AI agents ask them to approve?
:page-faq-4-a: Mostly not. Anthropic found that users of Claude Code accept 93% of its permission prompts, and named the result approval fatigue. The pattern predates AI: a 2013 study of 25 million browser security warnings found users clicked through 70.2% of one browser's certificate warnings. Chapter 14 of The Blueprint argues that human approval belongs only at the irreversible points, where each moment of attention counts.
:page-faq-5-q: How reliable does an AI agent need to be before it works unsupervised?
:page-faq-5-a: More reliable than a single good result suggests. Anthropic's guide to testing agents shows that an agent with a 75% success rate on one attempt passes all of three attempts only about 42% of the time. That is why the book recommends setting a promotion bar in advance and running the agent in shadow first; at CorralData, one agent's playbook set the bar at seven days of shadow data. Chapter 14 of The Blueprint explains how autonomy is earned.
= By the Numbers

[.lead]
_Field data from a working team of people and agents, and the research behind the book_

[.in-short]
*In short:* In a Google Research study, agents working in parallel without a coordinator amplified errors 17.2 times, against 4.4 times with one. At CorralData, about ten team bots appeared in two weeks, and two QA bots were competing for the same work by the end of the first day. Agents multiply fast; structure keeps their errors contained.

This page collects the figures from the chapters of _The Blueprint_ in one place. Each one links to the chapter where it appears, so you can read it in context before you quote it.

There are two kinds. The first is field data: counts and durations from CorralData, the small healthcare analytics company where I am CTO, where a team of people and a growing number of agents work side by side in Slack. The second is published research that the book cites, with the original source for every figure. Quote the second kind as research. Quote the first as what it is: one company's experience, observed by someone inside it. It is a case, not a survey, and nothing here claims that other organisations will see the same numbers.

== The Two Sets of Numbers Point the Same Way

The research and the field data were gathered for different reasons, and they agree.

The research says agents without a coordinator amplify one another's errors, and that multi-agent failures cluster in system design, misalignment between agents and missing verification. The field data shows what that looks like in a small company: two bots doing the same job on day one, an empty profile nobody removed, an alert that went to the one person who could fix it and still went unfixed. None of those was a model failure. Each was a gap in who owned what.

The research says people approve almost everything put in front of them. The field data shows the other side: a pipeline that moved its own work forward again minutes after a person moved it back. A human check only works where the system respects it.

The research says reliability compounds downwards: three in four on a single attempt becomes about two in five across three. The field data shows the bar a playbook set against that arithmetic, seven days of shadow data, and how easily it was cut short. Bars set in advance are what keep the arithmetic honest.
